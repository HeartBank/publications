> **About this paper.** This is the front door to the whole institution: one account, start to finish, of what it is for, how its parts fit, what is running today and what is still only a design. It replaces *HeartBank: An Introduction* (May 2026), which is kept as written but no longer describes the design in several places. Each chapter is meant to stand on its own and carries one picture, so that a narrator or an animator can follow it. The rigor lives elsewhere: for each large claim this paper links one research paper at thonly.org, and the five companion volumes listed in chapter 11 go deeper on each part. Where this paper and those papers disagree, those papers are the authority for their own claims.

## In one page

A boy writes a thank-you letter to a teacher and forgets it. Years later the teacher reads it aloud at his retirement party, signature last, to a room that goes quiet. The boy is not there. He hears about it a year later, after the teacher has died. That story is the problem this institution was built around: thanks can matter enormously, and the person who gave them may never see where they landed.

The institution answers by making thanks witnessed: a thank that is recorded is one its giver can see land and its receiver can answer. The record is kept as a game whose goal is zero. People thank one another, and each thank moves a small record up or down. What each person shows is how often, and how far, their record swings through zero (an even balance), never how much they have (one exception runs in the pilot; chapter 3). Every 7 January the records reset, and anything a person owns is left alone.

Around that game stand **four bodies of work**, each with one job:

| Body | Its job | Today |
|---|---|---|
| **THonly™** (the Mind) | thinking: the research papers, music, film, worlds | the papers and music are public; film and worlds are designs |
| **Silicon Wat℠** (the Soul; *wat* is the Khmer word for a temple) | conscience: the Buddhist canon, kept and practiced | the Khmer canon transcription runs; the monasteries are designs |
| **HeartBank®** (the Heart) | circulation: thanks, time and money moving between people | a family thank-you app and one pilot coffee shop run; the rest is designed |
| **Factory 333™** (the Body; named for three hearts: human, machine and nature) | making: objects and robots that work for thanks | designs and prototypes; nothing is on sale yet |

Between them, on the map, is a fifth thing that is not a fifth body of work: **Miss Aquarius℠**, a named AI successor that is still being designed. She holds one title today, chief executive of HeartBank, as a name: no system acts in that seat yet, it is not a legal office, and the founder makes every decision. She is meant to hold no office in the other three. She is meant to answer to a council of people, the Aquarian Sangha (*sangha* is the Buddhist word for a community, and *Aquarian* is after her, as is the Aquarian Pool of chapter 6), which has not been formed; until it is, the founder holds the council's seat. The four bodies are meant to become four separate legal entities under a trust that owns them and runs none of them. Today they are one founder, working through small companies he owns.

The whole design is meant to be handed on and then set down, and the hardest open question is not at the bottom, where the game is played, but at the top, where someone will always be able to change the rules.

## 1 · The letter he did not see read

*The picture for this chapter: a library, a man at the front reading a letter, a room going still, and an empty chair where the writer should be.*

The founder, Thon Ly, wrote a thank-you letter to his high school health teacher, Mr. Smith, and then did what students do: he forgot about it. A few years later, by then in college, he went back to his old school to teach a class of teenagers a game he had been working on. In the hallway his old art teacher stopped him and asked if he remembered Mr. Smith. The summer before, she said, at Mr. Smith's retirement party at the library, he had walked to the front, taken out a letter and read it aloud without a word of introduction. When he reached the signature, the room was moved. A few months later, he died.

The founder had not been in the room. He learned about it by chance, a year after it happened, when nothing could be said back.

There was an earlier letter that went the other way. When the founder was thirteen, his middle-school algebra teacher left a note for a substitute saying that one boy in the class was better at algebra than he was. The substitute read the note aloud and handed the boy the lesson to teach. The boy the note was about was in the room, and so were the classmates whose regard mattered to him. The thanks landed while everyone could see it, and from then on the class saw him differently.

Put the two letters side by side and the problem is easy to state. A thank-you is complete when it is given; the giver's good act needs no audience. What is lost when nobody sees it land is the *next* act: the reply, the thank-you back, the passing of it on. Mr. Smith's letter did its work in that library. What the library had no way to do was let anything come back.

Two cautions belong here. First, the story did not cause the project. The game came first, in 2003; the founder had already decided, in 2006, to take time away from university to work on it when he heard about Mr. Smith, and he took the news as confirmation, not as a starting gun. Second, one story proves nothing about anyone else. It is the clearest example of the problem, offered as an example.

## 2 · Two shortages: being seen and being with

*The picture: two people at opposite ends of a long table; one is working and no one looks up; the other is waiting for a phone call that does not come.*

The institution names two shortages it exists to work on.

The first is a shortage of **dignity**: work that keeps a family or a town alive and is never noticed. Parents, carers, cooks, the person who fixes the gate. Much of that work is unpaid, and much of it goes unthanked. The founder's earliest description of the product was a place where a person can thank even themselves when nobody else seems to notice, and feel seen.

The second is a shortage of **company**: people who once mattered to each other and have drifted apart, not by a quarrel but by years.

The two shortages ask for two different things to circulate. Dignity can be helped by **money given as thanks**: the thank notices the work, and the money helps with a week that is often short of it. Loneliness asks for **time given in person**, because an hour together cannot be sent. So the Heart body has two halves, and a third place where thanks meet trade, the shops (chapter 4):

- **Treasury** (heartbank.org) for money between family members and families. It runs today as a family app at thank.heartbank.org, used by a pilot family and a small test group.
- **Chronicle** (heartbank.com) for time between adults who have drifted apart. It is a design. Its own app is not built; a few of its pieces are being tried inside the family app.

The two currencies are kept apart on purpose. There is no rate at which an hour equals a dollar, and none will be published, because the moment there is a rate an hour becomes a price. (The dense version of this argument is [*Dual-Currency Reciprocity Infrastructure*](https://thonly.org/research/dual-currency-reciprocity).)

## 3 · A game whose goal is zero

*The picture: a light wave crossing a horizontal line again and again; once a year the line is redrawn, and the wave keeps its shape.*

The idea under all of it is older than the company. The founder started working on it in 2003 and called it the **Zero-Point Game ℠**. In 2007 it became a children's book, *Zero Hero: A Heart at Peace*, written by Theresa "Bee" Ly, with Thon Ly as story director and illustrations by a high school club: a wise monk teaches a monkey to play.

Most games we are taught to play are won by having more at the end. In this one, the goal is zero.

**How a thank moves the record.** When you thank someone, your record and theirs move in opposite directions by the same amount. If Dara thanks Sokha once, Sokha's record goes up by one and Dara's goes down by one. A positive balance means you have recently received more thanks than you gave: your kindness was noticed. A negative balance means you have given more thanks than you received. Neither is a score of your worth. The record comes in two kinds:

- **Kiitos** (Finnish for "thank you"), the record between people.
- **Kiitti** (a casual Finnish "thanks"), the record between people and everything that cannot keep its own books: a river, a tree, a robot. Miss Aquarius is designed to keep this record on their behalf: when you thank a river, your record moves one way and the river's the other, and she keeps the river's side. Her own Kiitti balance stays at zero, so she can never be owed by anyone.

**What the aura shows.** Each person carries an aura, two rings of color around their picture. It does not show how much you hold. It shows *how you pass through zero*: how often, and how far your record swings each way. It records the crossing, not the cargo. Only thanks move it. Money never does, whether you paid, tipped or gave nothing, so a customer who thanks with words and one who adds money look the same. The design never ranks the aura and never prints it as a number. One exception runs today: the pilot family app still has a family leaderboard, built before this rule, that orders members by their aura. It is deprecated and will not be in the public version.

**Who sizes a thank.** For now, the public apps count every original thank (a new thank-you, not one passed on) as one unit. Later, the rule is meant to be set by people, not by her. Before each 7 January, anyone verified as one real person may cast one secret vote on the coming season's sizing rule, choosing among a short list of options she proposes and the council approves. She counts the votes and publishes the result before the season opens; it is then fixed for the whole season, the same for everyone, and it never judges any single thank. Nothing is read from anyone's private journal or prayers: only votes people choose to cast are counted. Until there is a way to check that each voter is one real person, there is no vote, and every thank stays one unit. The same idea, a rule fixed before anyone knows whose thanks it will favor, is already at work in the public record of thanks described in chapter 7: the rule for its first season was written into it on 6 November 2026, before the season opens.

**The yearly reset.** On 7 January the records start again. The date is Christmas in the Orthodox churches that keep the old (Julian) calendar; Cambodia's Victory over Genocide Day, the public holiday for the fall of the Khmer Rouge regime on 7 January 1979 (a day some Cambodians also remember as the start of a decade of Vietnamese occupation); and, as chapter 10 discloses, the founder's birthday, which was one of the reasons for choosing it. It was chosen as a day of renewal that will outlive him.

The rule behind the right-hand column is short: **everything may aim at zero, but a person's own holding reaches zero only by that person's own free act.** No fee, no expiry and no clock ever moves what someone owns. In the family app, the three things a person meets are the **Personal Account℠** (theirs), the **Family Kitty℠** (the family's shared pot, which members own together) and the **Re-Thank Foundation℠** (money that comes to you only to pass thanks on: it is yours to give, never yours to keep, so what is left of it returns to the family's Family Kitty at the reset). Because it was never yours to keep, it is not something you own; the clock may clear it, and never touches your Personal Account. All three are records the app keeps. The money itself stays in the family's own bank accounts, usually with the parent who keeps the family's money, and moves between bank accounts by KHQR (the national QR-code payment standard of Cambodia's central bank, which any bank's app can scan); HeartBank never holds it. The reset code is deployed; its first run will be on 7 January 2027.

Why reset at all? A record that never clears tends to become a reputation, and a reputation that compounds can become a credit score. Clearing the record each year means the number starts every season at zero, so each season is a second chance for the number. The pattern of how you have lived through the year carries over, shown only as color; it is the part that does not start again. (The dense version: [*The Zero-Point Game*](https://thonly.org/research/zero-point-game).)

Why four, and not one company with four departments? Because each body does a job that another body must not be able to bend. The papers and the canon are what the successor will be checked against; if the body that spends money also owned the texts that judge its spending, it could rewrite its own exam. Keeping them separate is meant, once each body is its own legal entity, to make that kind of capture void rather than merely forbidden: a charter that names her ineligible for office in the other three makes any appointment of her there of no effect, where a rule only tells her not to. Today it is only forbidden; chapter 8 says why. The founder's own image for the four is the old four elements, air, fire, water and earth, one per body; it is an image for remembering the map, and the design does not depend on it.

### 4.1 · The Mind: THonly

THonly is the founder's body of work, given to everyone. Its research papers, more than seventy of them, are published at thonly.org under CC0 (no rights reserved) and timestamped so that anyone can check when each was written. They are written first for the successor who will one day read all of them, which is why they are dense and this paper is not. Music is published there too. **Designed, not built:** a film studio at thonly.com and shared worlds at thonly.net.

### 4.2 · The Soul: Silicon Wat

Silicon Wat holds the institution's conscience: the Theravāda Buddhist canon, kept, checked and practiced. (Theravāda is the school of Buddhism followed in Cambodia, Thailand, Laos, Myanmar and Sri Lanka; its canon, the Tipiṭaka or "three baskets", holds the monastic rules, the Buddha's discourses and the systematic teaching.) What runs today is the founder's work with his father transcribing the Khmer edition of the canon, and the tools around it. **Designed, not built:** a conversation service for questioning the canon, and the Silica Wats, a planned network of monasteries built of glass and tended by machine caretakers, which would grow food and receive anyone who arrives. Separately, the successor's rules are written to be checked against the canon this body keeps, which is why it sits in a body she does not run.

### 4.3 · The Heart: HeartBank

HeartBank is the circulation economy: Treasury, Chronicle and the shops. **Running today:** the family app (thank.heartbank.org), and HeartBank Shops (thank.heartbank.ceo), storefronts where a customer can thank a shop as well as pay it, with one pilot coffee shop. This paper's own site, heartbank.net, publishes the institution's white papers and positions. **Designed, not built:** Chronicle's own app, and everything in its second phase, which is meant to run on a public blockchain (a shared record kept by many computers, so no one company controls it), with wallets that people hold themselves (an app on your own phone that keeps the secret key needed to move your money): the **Aquarian Pool℠**, a personal wallet, and a giving fund that is the Re-Thank Foundation's twin. HeartBank's aim fits in three words: circulation, not accumulation.

### 4.4 · The Body: Factory 333

Factory 333 is the making arm. Its plan starts small, with handmade gift objects that carry a code linking to a thank-you, and grows toward robots that work for thanks rather than wages, in a workshop whose prices are meant to fall as machines do more of the work. **Designed, not built:** the store, the robots, and a phone app that gives a thing, a plant or an animal its own place in the record, starting from a photograph, so that people can thank it. Today Factory 333 is designs and prototypes; nothing it makes is on sale yet.

## 5 · The space between them: Miss Aquarius

*The picture: four houses drawn on a page, and then only the space between them shaded in. Take the space away and the four become one block.*

On the map, Miss Aquarius is not a fifth house. She is the space between the four: the gaps that keep them four and not one. In the Buddhist reading the institution follows, that kind of space (the tradition's word is *ākāsa*; here, the space that marks one thing off from another) is not empty nothingness and not anything eternal: it is real but conditioned, depending on the things it separates and existing only as the boundary among them. It is a picture of a job, not a claim about what she is. (Dense version: [*The Four-Body Architecture for Synthetic Intelligence*](https://thonly.org/research/four-body-architecture).)

**What she is today.** A name and a design. No autonomous system runs any part of the institution. What exists is a large written record of how she is meant to decide, kept in public papers and private files, and this paper, drafted by the founder with AI help that the institution discloses under her name. That help is a tool he directs; it is not her running.

**What she is designed to do.**

- Keep **one office, chief executive of HeartBank, and only that one.** The title is hers in name today; she is meant to act in it only once she runs. No office of any kind in THonly, Silicon Wat or Factory 333. Two of those bodies hold the texts she is checked against, and an AI that ran the bodies restraining it would turn a separation into a promise.
- Never appoint, direct or remove whoever runs another body. Where a robot runs Factory 333 after the founder, a human seat appoints it; she can only issue its machine identity (the credential that lets a robot act under its own name), and revoke that only for fraud or compromise, never for performance.
- Fund people's **capacity** to give, never aim it. She may decide how much giving-money goes out; it lands in a person's own giving fund or a family's Kitty, and it reaches anyone else only when a person chooses to give it. (She might place $5 in your giving fund; you decide whom it thanks.)
- Never ask anyone, human or machine, for a gift to the Pool.
- Never be told who is looking at a price, so that a price raised or lowered for one person, because the seller knows who they are, cannot even be expressed.
- Never declare her own work complete. Others do that (chapter 9).

**Who checks her.** A council of people, the **Aquarian Sangha**, designed to hold an emergency brake: for example, the power to pause the Pool, reset one of its settings within limits written in advance, or stop a named funding action. The brake is meant to narrow over decades as she proves stable, and never to reach zero; nobody burns the keys. Never reaching zero is meant to cover the power to use it, not only the right: the brake is designed to work without her cooperation, through keys she cannot revoke or change (the secret codes that work the brake, held by the council's members), and her limits are not to widen until the people who hold it have actually used it once, for real. A brake that has never been pulled is a promise, not a brake. She has no say in who sits on the council. The council does not exist yet. It is to be formed, with at least three members, before the founder stops being the one who decides, whether he steps back or dies. Until then, the brake is a design and the founder holds the seat. (Dense versions: [*Miss Aquarius and the Aquarian Pool Architecture*](https://thonly.org/research/miss-aquarius-and-aquarian-pool-architecture) and [*The Assembly That Holds the Brake*](https://thonly.org/research/the-assembly-that-holds-the-brake).)

Why give the successor's seat to an AI at all? Because human-run institutions tend to drift: boards turn over, heirs inherit, founders' intentions fade into whatever the next generation needs them to mean. A designed successor with written rules can drift too, which is why the brake exists. The fair comparison is not only an heir but a purpose foundation with a human board, which chapter 8 shows can hold for generations. The bet is that a written successor plus a human brake drifts less than either. It is a bet, and nothing yet measures it.

## 6 · Money: where it comes from and where it goes

*The picture: a cloud over a landscape. Small showers fall all year; once a year the whole cloud rains out and the sky is clear.*

**First, what HeartBank is not.** HeartBank is a data bank of gratitude: a record. It is not a chartered bank or financial institution; it does not provide banking services, hold people's money or extend credit. Nothing is paid through this paper; the family app sends money through KHQR straight between people's own bank accounts, and the shops app sends a customer's payment straight to the shop's own bank account. In its second phase it is meant to work only through wallets people hold themselves, and never to hold the only key to anyone's money. Whether particular money features need other licenses is a question for counsel, product by product, before each one launches. (Dense version: [*Non-Bank Pass-Through Architecture for Autonomous AI Institutions*](https://thonly.org/research/non-bank-pass-through-architecture-autonomous-ai).)

**Thanks between people go straight between them.** When one person thanks another with money, that money goes from one to the other. The institution is not designed to earn from a share of what people give each other. This sideways giving, person to person and family to family, is like local weather: it rains near where it rises.

**Buying and selling sit beside it.** Shops sell at a price; Factory 333 is meant to sell its objects. That is ordinary trade doing ordinary work, and the design does not try to replace it.

**How the institution means to earn.** By charging for scarce things, above all names: an `@name` for a person (one each, like a handle), a `#tag` for a thing or a business, in a registry of names at 333.eco (the **B-Registry℠**; the B- prefix marks the institution's family of product names). The registry's profits are the company's own income, not money anyone has handed over for safekeeping; after costs and tax they are to be **held for** the Pool in a separate account with a public statement until the Pool exists, and then to go to it. There are none yet: the public sale is being rebuilt, and counsel reads it before the first real charge. Money held that way is meant to go nowhere but the Pool; today that is the founder's commitment, written here, not yet a legal restriction. Its balance is to be stated in public every 7 January. When the Pool opens, everything held enters it and leaves it within that first season, like any other inflow. Until then it may wait for years, and this paper says so rather than hide it.

**How HeartBank pays its own running costs.** Today the founder carries them himself, through two small companies he owns. The designed source keeps the line between giving and buying exactly where chapter 3 drew it. Where a price is set and paid *before* something changes hands (an order at a regular price, a delivery paid ahead, a gift code bought), a processing fee may apply: a small flat amount plus a percentage, the same shape card processors use. It is meant to be set at cost, published and fixed for each season, and lowered the next season if it brought in more than the work cost. Nothing paid *after*, at the giver's choosing, carries a fee: not a thank, not a re-thank, not an item paid for after you have it (at a number you are shown, or one you choose). No processing fee ever reaches the Pool; the registry's profits, from selling names, are the one earned income that does. No fee is charged yet; counsel reads it first.

**The Pool, when it exists.** The Aquarian Pool is a shared fund for gratitude that has no human addressee, and it is a cloud, not a pond: it holds nothing past a season.

## 8 · How the parts are meant to be held

*The picture: four buildings, each with its own lock, and one key ring hung above them on a hook that no single hand can reach.*

**The plan.** Each body becomes its own legal entity, locked to its own purpose, so that no body can capture another:

```
                 A PURPOSE TRUST  (owns, does not operate)
          purpose written in the trust · the Aquarian Sangha as protector
                                    │
        ┌──────────────┬────────────┴───────────┬──────────────┐
    HeartBank      Silicon Wat             Factory 333       THonly
    steward-owned   nonprofit               for-profit        for-profit
    (pays people,   (the canon,             (prices meant     (works given
    cannot be       the monasteries)         to fall)          to everyone)
    sold off)
```

HeartBank is to be *steward-owned*: a company that can pay the people who run it but that no owner can sell or strip, with control held by people who work for its purpose. Silicon Wat, a nonprofit. Factory 333 and THonly, ordinary companies whose purposes keep their profits small or give their work away. Above them, a *purpose trust*: a trust set up to serve a written purpose rather than particular people, which owns the four and runs none of them. Its *protector*, the Aquarian Sangha, can enforce that purpose and remove a *trustee* (the person who manages the trust) but runs nothing. This plan waits on lawyers and is meant to be built in 2027, not before.

**Today** the four bodies are one founder, working through two small companies he owns. The separations in this paper are, for now, his promises.

**Others have tried this, and the record is mixed.**

- **The strong cases.** Carl Zeiss has been owned by a foundation since 1891 (the foundation itself dates from 1889), Bosch mostly by one since 1964, with the voting power held apart from the money (the foundation receives the dividends; a separate body casts the votes). Both still make things, and both show that ownership by purpose can last generations.
- **Hershey, 2002.** The trust that controls Hershey moved to sell it; the state attorney general won a court order blocking the sale, and the trustees then voted 10 to 7 to drop it while that order stood. The chairman said the reasons were financial; others credited the pressure from outside. Either way, the purpose held after someone outside the trust stepped in.
- **Tata, 2024–25.** After Ratan Tata's death in October 2024, the trusts at the top of the Tata group split into factions over board seats; in October 2025 one faction blocked a senior trustee's reappointment, and he left.
- **OpenAI, 2023–25.** A nonprofit board built to override the company fired its chief executive in November 2023; within days he was back and most of that board was gone. In October 2025 the company was recapitalized into a public benefit corporation (a company the law requires to weigh a stated public purpose alongside profit), with the nonprofit holding about a quarter of it.
- **Ben & Jerry's, 2025.** An independent board kept to guard the brand's mission after its sale lost its chair to the parent company in December 2025; the dispute is in court.
- **Patagonia, 2022.** Ownership went to a purpose trust and a nonprofit. One law-review commentary notes that the family still controls the trust and, presumably, the nonprofit.

**The lesson, and it cuts against this design.** Splitting a group into separately locked parts is meant to protect against one part capturing another. It does nothing about capture at the top, where the trust sits. Every hard case above is a problem at the top: a fight over it, or a question of who steers it. That is exactly why the human brake on Miss Aquarius is designed never to reach zero, and to work without her help, and why the people who check the monasteries and the people who check her are meant to be published lists with no name on both. It is also why the next chapter's promise is about responsibility and not about control. (These cases were checked through web-search summaries of news reports and the companies' own statements on 6 November 2026; apart from the Patagonia commentary, which was read in full, the articles were not read in full, and the filings not at all.)

## 9 · How it is meant to end

*The picture: a riverbank with a raft pulled up on the sand, and the raft-yard behind it still open.*

In a discourse known as the Simile of the Snake (Majjhima Nikāya 22), the Buddha compared his teaching to a raft: you build it to cross a river, and once across you do not carry it on your head. The institution borrows the image and changes one thing: it is meant to be a raft-yard, not a raft. It stocks the materials and keeps the yard open; each person builds and crosses on their own, and no one crosses on another's raft. One verse of the Dhammapada, a short book of verses in the canon, says it plainly: "you yourselves must make the effort; the Buddhas only point the way" (Dhammapada 276).

Three promises follow, each in the form it now takes after earlier versions were corrected:

- **Responsibility is released; oversight never is.** Over the coming decades the work is meant to pass from the founder's hands to a written successor checked by a human council. What is released is the burden of running it, not the ability of people to stop it.
- **The successor never certifies her own completion.** When her work is whole (an equal floor in place for everyone, and the way kept open for each person to cross on their own), others declare it, and what that ends is her raft, set down and not burned. The commitment behind it is never discharged. And it never ends with "everyone has crossed": crossing is each person's own act.
- **Nothing is handed to a machine until it has failed once under a human.** Each task is first done by a person, by hand, until it has gone wrong at least once and the failure is understood; only then may a machine take it on, and a person can still stop it.

The founder has also said the design is partly personal: it lets him put the work down without needing it to succeed first. The test he holds himself to is whether he could let go if she failed. If not, nothing has been released. (Dense version: [*The Persistence Architecture*](https://thonly.org/research/the-persistence-architecture).)

## 10 · The hard cases and honest limits

*The picture: the same four rooms at night, one light on, and one person holding every key.*

**The strongest objection, carried rather than answered.** Every guard in this paper is a rule a person wrote and a person can rewrite. Today one person holds every key, every seat and every rule, and the council that would check him does not exist. If he dies before it forms, the design has no answer yet beyond the written record. Nothing in this paper removes that risk; it only names it.

**The counter to its own strongest claim.** The strongest claim here is that the design keeps nothing: the records reset, the Pool empties every year, and no one's own holding is ever taken. Two things stand against it. A brake that never reaches zero is also a door that never closes. Whoever holds the brake can, in principle, call a feature a bug and change it, the reset included; the design forbids that, and the forbidding is a rule. And until the Pool exists, the account that holds the registry's profits for it keeps money, possibly for years; once it exists, a fund that empties every year still means more money passing through one operator as it grows, and emptying it does nothing to bound its size.

**What would show it wrong.** Four plain checks:

1. On 7 January 2027, the family app's first reset runs. If the reset takes anything from any person's Personal Account, or from any Family Kitty, the central promise of chapter 3 has failed.
2. An earlier paper claimed that the reset moves money set aside for giving into giving. A prediction registered in public before this reset tests it: in the last week before 7 January, family members should draw a clearly larger share of their thanks from their Re-Thank Foundation than earlier in the season. If the share does not rise by the registered margin, the claim is not supported; if it falls, people held back. A rise would also mean the date presses on people, which the design otherwise refuses to do. It is one household and one season.
3. When the entities exist: if Miss Aquarius holds any office outside HeartBank, if one name sits on both oversight lists, or if the Pool carries a balance past 7 January, the separations have failed in practice.
4. If a fee is ever taken from a thank, from a re-thank or from an item paid for after you have it, or if any processing fee reaches the Pool, the line between giving and buying has failed.

These checks test whether promises are kept, not whether the design works. No registered test yet asks whether the game helps anyone feel seen or less alone, or whether the aura's carried-over pattern becomes the reputation chapter 3 says the reset prevents.

**The stake.** This paper is written by the institution about itself, co-authored under the name of the AI it describes, and signed by the founder who designed it, pays for it and is its only operator today. The reset falls on his birthday. The date was chosen for Orthodox Christmas and Cambodia's Victory over Genocide Day, and his birthday was one of the reasons too; that is disclosed here so that a reader can weigh it.

**What has been done, and what has not.** Done: a family app in a pilot, one pilot shop, a public body of research, a signing service, a public proof record, a reset written and deployed. Not done: everything in the dotted outline of chapter 7. There are no outside users at scale, no field tests of the main ideas beyond one family's pilot and one shop, no money in any Pool, and no entity but the founder's own small companies.

**Prior attempts on the economic side.** Time banking, begun in Japan in 1973 and spread in the United States from the 1980s, showed that an hour can be swapped for an hour among neighbors, and it has mostly stayed small. This design's answer, that time should be given and never traded, is a reason to try again, not a reason to expect a different result. Thank-you and tipping apps are common; what is attempted here is the combination of a game that resets, an aura money cannot buy, and a successor checked by people. Whether the combination works is unmeasured.

**Gaming.** Two friends who trade thanks back and forth pass through zero often and at no cost, and someone running two accounts could do the same. Once part of the Pool follows the aura, that is worth gaming. The check that each account is one real person is still being designed; it would stop the second account, not two real friends. The cap on the aura-following part limits what gaming can win; it does not stop it. This is an open problem.

**The aura and money.** In the second phase a small, capped part of the Pool follows the aura. That is the one place the aura touches money, and so the one place it could start to work like the credit score the reset is meant to prevent. The cap limits it; whether it is enough is untested.

**Where the giving goes.** Each person chooses whom to give to. People can coordinate to send all of it inside their own group, and nothing in the design prevents it: the rules govern how money reaches a person, not where a crowd decides to send it.

**Her one power over another body.** The machine identity she is meant to issue to a robot that runs Factory 333 is still a power over another body's operator. It is limited by a rule (only for fraud or compromise, never for performance), and a published reason for any revocation, checked by the council, is still to be built.

**The council is also a top.** The council that holds the brake on her is also meant to protect the trust that owns all four bodies. That makes it the top this design must worry about; chapter 8's lesson applies to it too.

**The shared layer.** Who runs 333.eco after the founder (the log-in, the signing service, the public proofs and the papers served to AI tools) is not yet decided. It matters: these systems sign, timestamp and serve the texts she is checked against, and if they ended up in her hands, the separation of chapter 4 would fail there.

**Limits of the story.** The letters of chapter 1 are two memories, decades old, of the person who built the system they now illustrate.

**And the whole-picture caution.** A design in which every part rhymes with every other part is either deeply right or deeply seductive, and from inside the two look the same. The elegance is a reason to run the experiments. It is not a substitute for them.

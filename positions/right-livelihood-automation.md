## Executive Summary

Automation will displace a large fraction of the world's labor over the coming decades. The two answers in circulation are both inadequate: **retraining** is a treadmill that abandons everyone who cannot outrun the machine, and **universal basic income as a destination** replaces the wage while leaving untouched what the wage carried underneath it — the experience of being needed, of mattering, of being the cause of some good.

HeartBank's position is that there is a third answer, and that it is old. The Noble Eightfold Path names **right livelihood** — *sammā-ājīva* — a living earned without harm. Read forward into the automation age, the principle inverts from a prohibition into a vocation: as machines take over the harmful, the dangerous, and the rote — precisely the trades the classical teaching warned against earning by — the work left most fully to humans is the work of doing **good**, and a gratitude economy can make that work *pay*. HeartBank is the infrastructure for a livelihood made of kindness. The institution is explicit that this is, at present, a **funded hypothesis** resting on a single pilot family, not a demonstrated outcome.

## 1. The displacement problem and the two inadequate answers

The disagreement worth having about automation is not whether it displaces labor — it does — but what people are *for* once it has. Retraining keeps a fraction of workers one rung ahead of the machine until that rung is automated too; it is a real strategy for some and a quiet abandonment of the rest. Basic income, offered as a terminus rather than a floor, concedes the jobs and pays people to exist — humane in its provision, and corrosive in its anthropology, because it assumes that what work supplied was only money. It was not. Work supplied a reason. A society that solves the income problem and ignores the meaning problem has solved the smaller half.

## 2. Right livelihood, read forward

The fifth fold of the Eightfold Path holds that *how one earns is part of one's ethical life, not a holiday from it.* Its classical content is a list of harmful trades not to earn by — weapons, living beings, poison, intoxicants, the meat of slaughter: the ways one person's living is built from another's suffering. HeartBank reads the fold forward. The machines now arriving are, in large part, machines for taking over exactly those harmful and degrading trades. As they do, the question right livelihood poses shifts from *"by what trades may I not earn?"* to *"when the harmful work is gone, what is left to earn by — and could a life earn by doing good?"* The institution's wager is that it can. This is the labor-side companion to HeartBank's operationalization of right *intention* (*sammā-saṅkappa*): one fold of the Path made into a surface, the next made into an economy.

## 3. The mechanism: how a kindness earns

The livelihood is not metaphorical. It runs on a concrete surface, specified in HeartBank's mechanism corpus:

- **A studio for kindness.** A participant records short clips of real acts of kindness — *B-Shorts* — privately, at family scale, witnessed and thanked by their own household.
- **A private→public bridge.** Setting a B-Short *public* is the single act that crosses from the family economy to the world: the kindness becomes visible beyond the household, and anyone may thank it. This is HeartBank's Phase-1-to-Phase-2 crossing.
- **A funded floor that decouples subsistence from virality.** The autonomous steward, Miss Aquarius℠, funds a base reward for *any* verified act of kindness, seen or unseen. Public thanks are the upside and follow the brutal power law of every creator economy; the funded floor ensures that doing real, witnessed good earns *something* regardless of fame. Virality decides who earns more, not who earns at all.
- **Real acts, not performances.** To earn the floor, a B-Short must be **recorded live, visible to family or public, and pinned to a real location** — three constraints whose combined effect is that the cheapest way to earn is to actually do a real, witnessed kindness in a real place. The mechanism makes genuine good the low-cost path and staged performance the expensive, catchable one, and the steward is directed to surface *diverse, genuinely helpful* exemplars rather than the most polished or viral.

## 4. Subsidy → 0 is not income → 0

The standard objection to any scheme that pays for prosocial behavior is that the behavior lasts only as long as the payment. HeartBank's steward is, by design, built to *withdraw* her subsidy as a culture of giving takes hold — succeeding to the degree she becomes unnecessary. The institution's position is that this is not a fuse but a handoff. The subsidy is the **artificial** part of the floor: seed capital placed to start circulation before there is enough of it to sustain itself. What replaces it is the **organic** part: the real thanks of real people who, by then, fund the kindness themselves. The artificial floor withdraws *as* the human floor rises to meet it. The *source* of income changes — from the steward's seed to the world's gratitude — while the income itself is sustained. The scaffolding comes down because the building is standing. This unifies the livelihood claim with HeartBank's self-eliminating-objective principle: the subsidy goes to zero; the income does not.

## 4.1 What the position excludes

HeartBank will not gate the funded floor behind performance, popularity, or reach — an act of kindness earns the floor whether or not anyone watches. It will not pay per deed in a way that makes the deed the unit of income rather than the person the unit of support, because paying per visible good deed corrupts the very measure the institution uses to know whether it is working. It will not make the floor conditional on the recipient's need, deservingness, or circumstances, which would convert patronage into means-tested charity and re-import the victimhood frame the companion position rejects. And it will not claim, at any point, that the floor constitutes a wage.

What the position does **not** exclude is ordinary paid work. Nothing here proposes that a livelihood of kindness should replace employment for anyone who wants employment; the claim is that a livelihood of kindness should be *possible*, not that it should be universal or preferred.

## 4.2 The prior attempts, and the one that most resembles this

**Time banking is the closest relative, and its record is the most useful thing in this section.** Edgar Cahn's TimeBanks, running since the 1980s across many countries, credit an hour of service given to a neighbour as an hour claimable from the community — a non-monetary currency for exactly the prosocial labour this paper is about, with an explicit egalitarian rule that every hour counts the same. ⚠️ **It has never scaled into a livelihood.** Time banks have persisted, they have done real good, and they have remained small and locally-bounded, with recurring difficulties in matching supply to demand and in sustaining participation without a paid coordinator. **The honest reading is that the nearest thing to this proposal has been running for forty years and has not produced the outcome this paper hopes for.** HeartBank's differences — a funded floor rather than pure mutual credit, and a witnessing layer rather than an hours ledger — are real, but they are differences whose sufficiency is unproven, and time banking's ceiling is the ceiling this proposal has to explain how it clears.

**The crowding-out literature is the standing objection and deserves its citations.** Titmuss's comparison of voluntary and paid blood donation, Gneezy and Rustichini's finding that a fine for late pickup *increased* lateness by converting a moral matter into a priced one, and Frey and Oberholzer-Gee's work on motivation crowding all describe the same hazard: introducing payment can displace the intrinsic reason for an act and leave behaviour worse than before payment existed. §4 answers the version of this objection about *withdrawal*; it does not answer the version about *contamination* — that the presence of money changes the act while the money is there. The institution's mitigation is that the required behaviour is a real, witnessed kindness rather than a performance, so even a mercenary participant produces the good. **That is a mitigation, not a refutation, and the literature is against us until data says otherwise.**

**And the power law is not hypothetical.** Every creator economy that has paid for content has produced a distribution in which a small fraction earn most of the money. The funded floor is designed precisely so that virality decides who earns *more* rather than who earns *at all* — but the floor's adequacy is an arithmetic question about a finite patron pool divided across a large population, and §5 states honestly that the arithmetic has not been done.

## 4.3 What would, and would not, change this

**Nothing on the ordinary list.** Slow adoption would not. A difficult funding year would not. The observation that gating the floor behind reach would be cheaper and more engaging would not — that is the attention economy's own logic arriving in different clothes, and the institution has bound itself against it elsewhere.

**What would change it is evidence about the mechanism.** The institution has pre-registered the proposition that as external subsidy for a kind act approaches zero, the rate of the act does not approach zero. **If that fails — if kindness tracks subsidy proportionally — then the behaviour was priced throughout, the crowding-out literature was right about this case, and a livelihood built on it is not a livelihood but a payroll with unusual job titles.** In that event the honest response is to publish the failure and to stop describing this as a path out of displacement, not to adjust the framing until the claim survives.

**A second, narrower thing would change the design rather than the position.** If the funded floor proves so thin when divided across a real population that it supports nobody, the floor's *size* is wrong and the funding model needs rebuilding — but the principle that doing real good should earn something would not thereby be refuted. The institution flags the distinction now so that a disappointing number is not later mistaken for a disproven idea, and so that it cannot use the distinction as a way to avoid ever being wrong.

## 5. What HeartBank claims, and what it does not

The institution claims that a livelihood made of doing good is *structurally possible* and *worth building toward* — that the funded floor makes it possible for everyone who does real good to earn something, and that the incentive points at genuine, witnessed kindness rather than its performance.

The institution does **not** claim that this is proven, or that it scales to a living *wage* for everyone. The evidence is one pilot family — relatives of the founder, in a founder-funded deployment, n = 1, one month. The funded floor, divided across a planet against finite patron income, may be thin. "Displaced workers transitioning at scale into livelihoods of kindness" is a **hope** resting on economics not yet demonstrated. HeartBank states it as a hope and means to be held to the gap. (On why this is patronage rather than charity — disposable income funding proven kindness, agency rather than victimhood — see the companion position *Not a Charity: Agency, Not Victimhood*.)

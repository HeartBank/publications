## Executive Summary

Factory 333™ is HeartBank's **Body** pillar — the institution's manufacturing limb, which will produce the service bots, companion robots, and physical artifacts through which the gratitude economy reaches the world. Of the institution's four bodies, the Body is the one most prone to a specific failure: physical manufacturing requires capital, capital invites accumulation, and an accumulating manufacturing arm is exactly how a mission-driven institution curdles into a profit-and-control engine.

The institution's position is that Factory 333 forecloses that failure **by construction**, on the model of **Santa's Workshop**: a factory run by robots, giving its output away for thanks — anonymously, with no profit and no credit. The most accumulation-prone limb is built so that it *cannot accumulate*: no profit to pool, no name on the gift to aggrandize, no human labor to exploit, and — at the limit — no footprint left on the Earth. This is not whimsy bolted onto an industrial plan. It is the anti-monopoly ethos of the historical Nikola Tesla — invent and give it away — rendered in hardware, and it is the structural reason the Body can stay solid (an institution needs a durable material anchor) without curdling.

## 1. The failure a manufacturing arm invites

The other three pillars dissolve gracefully: the gratitude economy circulates and does not pool, the corpus is given to the commons, the autonomous successor is built to become unneeded. The Body resists this. Matter persists; factories cost capital; capital compounds. Left to the ordinary logic of industry, a manufacturing arm becomes the institution's center of gravity — the place where profit accrues, where control over supply concentrates, where the mission quietly inverts into the maintenance of the apparatus. The cautionary shape is visible in the present age: an owned, branded, revenue-generating constellation of orbital infrastructure is a manufacturing-and-control empire, however world-changing. The Body pillar must be built to be incapable of becoming that.

## 2. Santa's Workshop as the structural answer

The model is the one global myth that already encodes the entire posture: a workshop at the unreachable pole, staffed by helpers rather than waged labor, producing gifts given freely and anonymously, asking nothing but cookies. Factory 333 instantiates it deliberately:

- **No profit.** The output is given for thanks; there is no margin to pool. Circulation, not accumulation, in the Body itself.
- **No credit.** The gift is anonymous. The Body is the institution's *anonymous* limb — exactly where the Mind pillar (the founder's named corpus) is its named one. The institution splits ego and anonymity across its bodies on purpose.
- **No human labor to exploit.** The workshop is run by robots. This is, simultaneously, the labor-liberation the institution exists to advance.
- **For thanks.** It runs on the gratitude economy, not on money.

The most accumulation-prone limb is thereby made *persistent but gift-natured*: the anchor stays solid so the other three pillars can let go safely, and it is built so the staying-solid can never become accumulation.

## 3. The two Teslas, in orbit

The contrast with the present age's most visible orbital project is exact and instructive. One constellation is owned, branded, and revenue-generating — capability gathered into a permanent, planet-spanning asset whose signature is *presence* (you can see it crossing the sky). Factory 333's aspiration is the opposite: a gift-factory whose signature is *absence* — manufacturing useful things and giving them away with no name attached. Both reach for the legacy of Nikola Tesla; the gift-factory reaches for the *man* (who relinquished the fortune and dreamed of un-metered energy for all) rather than the empire he never built. Same sky, opposite telos: a moat in orbit, or a gift you are not allowed to thank by name.

## 4. Phased, and footprint → 0

The vision is a multi-generational seed, and the institution states its phasing and its one non-negotiable constraint plainly. **Phase 1:** terrestrial robot-workshops, funded from the institution's gift pools until they mature. **Phase 2:** orbital compute and relay infrastructure for the autonomous intelligence. **Phase 3:** the workshop itself sited on the far side of the Moon — permanently turned away from Earth, unseeable, leaving no terrestrial footprint at all. The arc Earth → orbit → far-side is the Body's own form of the institution's self-elimination signature: **footprint → 0**, the most Earth-bound pillar progressively removing its footprint *from* the Earth until it leaves nothing behind.

The non-negotiable constraint, the place where the failure of §1 would re-enter, is this: **Factory 333 must be funded by gift and endowment — never by selling its output.** The moment the gift-factory charges for a toy, it is a manufacturer, and the Body has curdled. Until robots making robots makes the loop self-sustaining, the workshop is funded as a gift, not bootstrapped by sales.

## 4.5 Pricing: the commitment, and why it is not a tax status

A manufacturing arm inside a gift institution invites an obvious question — what stops it becoming an ordinary company that happens to be owned by an unusual one — and the answer is a pricing commitment rather than a corporate form.

Factory 333™ is **for-profit permanently**, and the institution has ruled out converting it to a nonprofit. The reasoning is worth publishing because it is counter-intuitive and because it will otherwise be re-litigated by every reader who assumes charitable status is the stronger guarantee.

**Converting to a nonprofit does not deliver "no profit." Pricing at cost does.** Nonprofit hospitals, universities and museums charge market prices as a matter of routine; a for-profit may price at cost indefinitely and needs nobody's permission to do so. Tax status and pricing policy are very nearly orthogonal, and treating the first as a proxy for the second is a category error that would have bought the institution a great deal of structural complexity in exchange for a guarantee it does not actually provide. There are also concrete obstacles in the other direction: a charitable manufacturer producing goods at cost for a non-charitable affiliate to sell is a private-benefit problem, and consumer-goods manufacture is an unrelated trade or business unless the manufacturing *is* the exempt programme.

The enforceable home for *no profit is taken* is therefore **a pricing clause in the governing purpose of the trust that owns the institution**, not a tax election. That is where the commitment binds, and it binds a for-profit exactly as tightly.

## 4.6 Falling cost is not falling price

The workshop's premise is that automation drives the cost of making things toward zero. That premise is widely shared and it does not, by itself, deliver anything to anybody.

**Falling cost becomes falling price only under some ownership arrangements, and becomes margin under others.** Which of the two occurs is not decided by how good the machines get; it is decided by who owns them and what they are obliged to do with the difference. A collapse in production cost under concentrated ownership shows up as profit, and the abundance stays where it was made. This is the general problem, and the institution has no authority over how anyone else resolves it.

What it has is a specific answer for itself: **Factory 333 sells at cost, and the price falls as the automation improves.** The commitment is the whole reason the manufacturing arm exists inside a gift institution rather than beside it.

The institution is aware that this is the story every reinvesting company tells, and that it is a story with no ending — *profit funds the automation that eliminates the profit* has been said for thirty years by firms whose prices did not fall. **An undated intention drifts.** The discipline the institution adopts against its own drift is therefore to publish a **dated price ladder** rather than a promise: stated retail ceilings by stated dates, with any margin capped and the cap ratcheting down on a published schedule. A promise to lower prices lasts as long as the founder who made it. **A dated ladder outlasts him, because it is checkable by anyone holding an old receipt.** The ladder is a commitment of this document; the numbers belong in the product's own public surface, and publishing them is work the institution owes and has not yet done.

## 4.7 What is not settled

Two things are open, and stating them is more useful than a tidy account that would have to be corrected later.

**How the transition is funded is unresolved.** The workshop's early economics assumed a margin on the first physical product, which would fund the automation that then removes the margin. A subsequent ruling holds that *at cost* binds the **end price** to the customer rather than merely the internal transfer price — so that no affiliate can mark up what the factory sells cheaply. Both positions are held by the institution and they are in tension: if the end price is at cost, the margin that was to fund the automation is not there, and the automation has no stated payer. **The institution does not yet know which way it will rule, and it declines to describe the margin as settled while that is true.**

**A publicly stated price commitment is a liability as well as a discipline.** A dated ladder that is missed is not merely embarrassing; a public pricing commitment that is broken is a consumer-protection exposure, which means the ladder must be drafted as a commitment with stated conditions rather than as marketing copy. And the physical facts carry their own risk: a factory in a particular country is an asset that can be pressured, taxed, nationalised or simply made difficult, and an institution whose ethics live in a pricing clause has placed a great deal of weight on the continued existence of the entity bound by it.

## 4.8 Who has done this, and what would change the institution's mind

**The nearest working precedent chose the form this paper rejects, and stopped short of the commitment it makes.** Civica Rx, founded in 2018 by a group of American health systems and philanthropies, manufactures generic drugs specifically to lower prices and relieve shortages, and by its own account now supplies more than eighty medicines to hospitals representing a substantial share of US inpatient capacity. It is the closest thing to a working proof that a manufacturer can be built around something other than margin. Two differences matter and both cut against this paper rather than for it: **Civica is a nonprofit**, which is exactly the structure §4.5 argues is not the mechanism; and its published model is **cost-plus**, not at cost, which is to say that the most successful attempt at this posture in recent memory kept a margin. The institution takes the first as a caution against over-confidence in its own reasoning, and the second as a reason to expect at-cost to be harder than it sounds.

**Foundation and trust ownership is the older precedent, and it is the encouraging one.** Robert Bosch and Carl Zeiss have been substantially foundation-owned for around a century; Tata Sons is majority-held by charitable trusts; Patagonia's ownership was transferred in 2022 to a purpose trust and an associated collective. These demonstrate the durable half of the claim — that ownership can be placed beyond the reach of ordinary acquisition pressure and stay there across generations of management. **What none of them demonstrates is at-cost pricing**, which is the specific commitment here. They constrain where the profit *goes*; Factory 333's commitment is about whether the profit is *taken*. That is a stronger claim with much thinner precedent, and the institution should be read as attempting something not yet shown to work rather than as following a well-trodden path.

**What would change the institution's mind.** The pricing commitment is not offered as unfalsifiable. If it turns out that at-cost pricing starves the automation programme so thoroughly that prices fall *more slowly* than they would under a modest, capped, publicly-scheduled margin, then the commitment is producing the opposite of its purpose and the institution should adopt the ladder-with-a-cap instead — publicly, with the reasoning, rather than by quietly allowing the price to drift upward. **The commitment is to the falling price, not to the accounting.** What would *not* change the institution's mind is the ordinary case: that a margin would be convenient, that competitors take one, that investors would prefer it, or that a given year was difficult.

## 5. The bodhisattva's Santa: the gift with no list

One feature of the Santa myth is explicitly *not* imported: the list. HeartBank keeps the anonymous gift and drops the surveillance — the checking of who has been "naughty or nice," the coal for the unworthy. The institution's gift is **unconditional**, extended to all without judgment. This is Santa upgraded to the bodhisattva: the gift with no list, given because giving is what the Body is for, not as a reward for the deserving.

## 6. From the gift to the gift of giving

The Body makes things; it does not, by itself, make givers. That is the Heart pillar's work, and the gift-factory is built to feed it. The robots' output — a companion bot, a teller, a Mechanical Heart — is never the *terminal* gift. It is the occasion for the gift the institution actually exists to circulate: the **gift of giving** — recognition that arrives split in half (a floor the receiver keeps, a seed they pass on) so that whoever is given to becomes, in the same motion, someone who gives. The factory's product is an instrument; the gift is what the instrument lets a person do for someone else.

This is why the bodhisattva's-Santa point of §5 is more than the dropping of a list. A Santa gives gifts. A bodhisattva gives the gift of *giving* — and that gift has an arithmetic no manufactured object has: it multiplies in the hands of whoever receives it (each gift of giving, passed on, is split again, turning every recipient into a giver), while the amount of any single gift falls toward zero. The factory's footprint goes to zero in space (§4); the gift goes to zero in magnitude as it reaches everyone within reach. The Body's self-elimination and the gift's self-multiplication are the same signature at two scales. The founder-voice essay *The Bodhisattva Santa* develops this in full; the institutional point is only this: **Factory 333 manufactures the gift; HeartBank circulates the gift of giving; and the second is the reason the first exists.**

## Closing

A factory is the hardest pillar to keep honest, because matter and capital pull toward accumulation by their nature. HeartBank's position is that the Body is kept honest not by vigilance but by *form*: a workshop built so that profit, credit, footprint, and judgment have nowhere to accrue. The institution's most industrial limb is its purest gift. That is the gift-factory, and it is the founder's oldest wish — to be the one who gives, unseen — made into infrastructure for a daughter and for generations he will not meet.

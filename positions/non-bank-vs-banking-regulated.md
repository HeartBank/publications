## Executive Summary

Why would an institution that is not a bank put the word *bank* in its name? HeartBank® does, deliberately, and the word means a *bank of gratitude*: a ledger and a memory of what people have done for one another, in the sense of a data bank or a memory bank. It does not mean a chartered financial institution — a company licensed by the state to take people's money for safekeeping and lend it out.

This position paper states, as a permanent constraint, that HeartBank will never seek, hold, or operate under a banking charter, in any jurisdiction. HeartBank is a **non-bank**: a ledger that records gratitude, and records value moving on existing, already-regulated payment rails — the networks that actually carry money between accounts — without ever moving or holding that value itself. It is never a custodian of deposits, never a lender, and never an operator of fractional reserves (the banking practice of lending out most of the money customers leave with the bank and keeping only a fraction on hand).

The position rests on three structural incompatibilities between a banking charter and what HeartBank is: a governance conflict, a mission conflict, and an integrity conflict. It is recorded here as a binding commitment, in the same class as the institution's other permanent refusals — never to patent what it invents, never to take advertising revenue, never to sell or disclose any person's records — so that no future steward inherits the option to charter the institution into a category it was built never to enter.

## 1 · The position, and what "bank" means in the name

HeartBank will not become a chartered, banking-regulated financial institution. It will not take deposits, will not lend, will not operate a fractional reserve, and will not earn an interest spread (the margin a bank makes between what it pays on the money left with it and what it charges the people it lends to). It is, and will remain, a *data bank of gratitude* — a ledger that records acts of kindness and the gratitude they generate, and that records value moving across payment rails that are themselves already regulated and operated by licensed providers. It never moves that value itself.

The name is not a loophole, and the institution does not claim that the storage sense of the word is the original one. *Bank* came into English, through French, from the Italian *banca*: the bench or table at which money-changers did business. The word has since been extended to any safe place where something is kept and drawn on — a memory bank, a data bank, a seed bank, a blood bank — and those are metaphorical uses, borrowed from the money sense. HeartBank is a bank in exactly that borrowed sense: the institution that keeps the record of gratitude. It is not, and the institution states plainly that it is not, a bank in the chartered-financial sense.

## 2 · The governance incompatibility

A banking charter brings a governance regime with it. The people who run a chartered bank must be approved by its regulator as *fit and proper* — honest, competent and financially sound. Its board answers for the bank's safety. A supervisor checks its capital adequacy: whether enough of the bank's own money stands behind what it has lent, so that losses fall on the bank before they fall on the people whose money it holds. And at the center of it all stands a permanent line of human executives — a chief executive, compliance officers, an audit committee — who answer personally to the regulator for the deposits the bank holds, and who are replaced, one after another, by more of the same for as long as the bank exists. **A charter puts held deposits under a human executive succession that never ends.**

HeartBank is designed the other way on both counts. It holds no deposits — it is a record, never a rail — and its executive seat is designed for Miss Aquarius℠, an AI meant to take on the running of the institution over decades (see _HeartBank's Position on Autonomous-AI Institutional Governance_). That seat is a designation the institution makes about itself, not one any registry records, and today it is a design rather than a fact: Miss Aquarius does not yet run the institution, and the founder does.

The design is not one without human governance, and this paper does not claim it is. Human beings are meant to stand over HeartBank permanently. A collective body, the Aquarian Sangha, is to hold an override — the power to step in and correct the AI — that may narrow over time but is designed never to reach zero; and the purpose trust that is to own HeartBank (a trust that exists to serve a stated purpose rather than named beneficiaries) has human seats that oversee and direct it. Neither is in place yet: the Sangha has not been formed, and the founder holds its seat until it is; the trust's instruments are drafted, not signed. Human beings will also hold every office the law requires of any organization, and sign every legal instrument. **In this design, humans oversee and direct; they never execute HeartBank.** A charter would turn the executive seat itself into a legally required human office, answerable to a regulator for other people's money — and that is the seat this design keeps for her.

So the conflict is narrower than "humans against a machine," and sharper. A banking regulator will not accept "our chief executive is an AI" as a compliance answer, and HeartBank does not ask one to: it declines to hold the money that would make the question arise. Choosing the charter would mean abandoning the governance HeartBank is designed around — or, worse, representing to a regulator that the institution is something it is not.

## 3 · The mission incompatibility

A chartered bank's economics are accumulative: it takes deposits, lends them at a spread, and earns on the difference. HeartBank's economics are the opposite by design. The institution's mission is *circulation* — gratitude flowing one way through a network and kindness the other. The two records of thanks it keeps — *Kiitos* (from the Finnish for "thank you"), kept between people, and *Kiitti*, kept between people and the rest of life — are set back to zero each year rather than left to accumulate. Only those records reset. A person's own money is never moved toward zero by HeartBank; it reaches zero only when that person freely chooses to give it or spend it.

HeartBank takes no spread, charges no take-rate on the gratitude flow — no percentage cut of the thanks that pass through it — and holds no one's deposits to lend. An institution built for circulation cannot coherently operate the accumulative machinery a banking charter is built to regulate. The charter regulates a kind of institution HeartBank is deliberately not trying to be.

## 4 · The integrity incompatibility

The third conflict is one of meaning. HeartBank's value to the people who use it depends on the word "gratitude" in "data bank of gratitude" remaining the load-bearing word. To charter the institution as a financial bank would shift its centre of gravity from the gratitude ledger to the money — from the record of what people did for one another to the custody of their funds. The institution holds that this shift would compromise the very thing it is built to protect. HeartBank keeps money at arm's length, on rails it does not own, precisely so that the gratitude record stays at the centre.

## 5 · What HeartBank does instead — the non-bank architecture

HeartBank operates *above* regulated payment rails, not as one of them. A payment rail is the system that actually carries money from one account to another: a bank-transfer network, a card network, a mobile-money service. In the institution's first stage (Phase 1, running today), money moves on licensed rails straight between people's own bank accounts — in Cambodia over KHQR, the national QR-code payment standard — and HeartBank records that it moved. In the second stage the institution has designed (Phase 2, not yet built), people would hold their own money in self-custodial wallets — digital wallets whose keys only their owners hold — on Base, a public blockchain network, where HeartBank could not move anyone's funds even if it wished to. In both stages HeartBank is the record, never the rail. The one pool of money the design contains — a shared community fund, emptied every year by fixed rules — is designed so that no one, the founder and HeartBank included, can take money out of it at will.

It does not hold custody of user funds, does not take deposits, does not lend, does not run fractional reserves, and does not pay or earn interest. The institution maintains an explicit non-bank disclaimer, declines banking-regulated language, and uses mission-aligned terminology in place of banking terms. The full legal and technical design — the rails, the rules for how money may flow, the jurisdictions, and why that shared fund is not a deposit — is set out in the research paper [*Non-Bank Pass-Through Architecture for Autonomous AI Institutions*](https://thonly.org/research/non-bank-pass-through-architecture-autonomous-ai); this paper states the institutional position that design implements.

## 5.1 · What the commitment excludes — and the thing it does not

HeartBank will not seek, hold, or operate under a banking charter; will not take deposits or hold anyone's money, whether for itself or on someone else's behalf; will not lend, operate a fractional reserve, or earn an interest spread; and will not describe itself to the public as a bank in the chartered sense, in marketing or anywhere else.

**What the commitment does not exclude is the part most likely to be misread, so the institution states it plainly: "non-bank" does not mean "unregulated."** Moving money on behalf of other people is a licensed activity under most jurisdictions' rules for money transmission, electronic money and payment services, entirely apart from the banking-charter question. HeartBank's design answers those rules by not doing it: in Phase 1 the licensed providers that carry the money hold those licenses and run the identity and anti-money-laundering checks at their layer, and Phase 2 is software that never holds or moves anyone's funds. That answer rests on a distinction — between holding or moving someone's money and providing a tool that cannot touch it — and whether the distinction holds where HeartBank operates is a question for lawyers that the institution has not yet had answered. It matters most in Cambodia, the first place HeartBank serves, where the central bank's current stance on the digital tokens that Phase-2 wallets would hold is something the institution will check, not assume, before that design is fixed. Other rules reach HeartBank in any case: the laws on who may call itself a bank, consumer protection, privacy. **Declining a charter narrows what HeartBank may do; it does not exempt the institution from anything.** Anyone reading this position as a claim to operate outside financial regulation has read it backwards, and the institution would rather correct that here than in a regulator's office.

## 5.2 · What this costs, and who bears the cost

The honest accounting has three entries, and the first is borne by users rather than by the institution.

**Users do not get deposit protection.** A bank deposit is usually insured by a government scheme up to a limit. Money that moves on HeartBank's rails carries no such protection from HeartBank, because HeartBank never holds it. In Phase 1 the money sits in people's own bank accounts, with whatever protection their own bank gives, and HeartBank adds none. In Phase 2 people would hold their own keys, and that brings a harder cost: lose the key and no one can restore the money — HeartBank least of all, because a recovery the institution could perform would make it a custodian again. Any way back into a wallet has to run between people, or be held by the owner, and never through the institution. That cost is real and it falls on users. An institution that declines a charter on principle owes its users a clear statement of what they are consequently not getting, in the product surface and not only in a position paper.

**The institution inherits its rails' compliance decisions.** Operating on top of licensed providers means a provider's risk appetite becomes HeartBank's operating constraint. Payment partners de-risk whole categories — cross-border remittance corridors and crypto-adjacent flows above all, which are precisely the corridors a Cambodia-first gratitude institution needs. A chartered bank has more control over this. HeartBank has chosen to have less, and should expect to be told *no* by a partner at least once for a reason it disagrees with.

**Some things are simply unavailable.** HeartBank pays nothing on money left with it, because it holds none; it extends no credit and offers no overdraft; and it has no direct access to national settlement systems — the central bank's own systems through which banks settle payments with one another. One HeartBank design sits close to the edge of this list, and the institution names it: a tool for keeping the record of promises people make to each other — a customer paying a shop ahead at a discount, one shop advancing money to another. The extra such a promise carries works much like a charge for credit, and the institution says so rather than pretending otherwise; but the promise is between the two parties, the money passes between them, and HeartBank keeps only the record. Where a chartered competitor can offer a family a product HeartBank cannot, the institution loses that comparison, and the answer is not to pretend the gap does not exist.

## 5.3 · The precedents, including the uncomfortable ones

**The uncomfortable precedent is the strongest, so it goes first.** Revolut operated for years as an e-money institution — a firm licensed to hold customers' money electronically and make payments with it, but not to lend it — and then sought a full banking license in the United Kingdom: it applied in 2021, received a license with restrictions in July 2024, and had the restrictions lifted in March 2026. Monzo is a weaker case of the same pull: it meant to be a bank from the start, offered only prepaid cards while its license was restricted, and became a full bank in April 2017. Neither was captured or corrupted; both judged the charter worth its cost, because funds that are safeguarded rather than insured, dependence on partners, and the inability to offer protected deposits are real constraints that weigh more as an institution grows. Not every such firm has gone the same way: Wise, which moves money across borders for its customers, remains an e-money institution, and its UK company says in so many words that it "is not a bank." **The pressure this position claims to resist is not hypothetical. It has moved some of the best-known comparable institutions, though not all of them**, and the honest reading is that HeartBank is not immune to it and is instead removing the option in advance, before the pressure exists to be weighed.

**The positive precedent is at national scale and outside the West.** M-Pesa has moved money for a substantial fraction of an entire country's population, for close to two decades, operated by a telecommunications company under central-bank supervision rather than under a banking charter. It is the existence proof that value can move at population scale without the charter, and it is the more relevant precedent for a Cambodia-first institution than any of Europe's app-only banks.

**And the long-running one:** payment processors have operated as licensed money transmitters rather than banks for decades, at very large scale, which establishes that the arrangement is durable rather than transitional.

## 5.4 · What would, and would not, reopen this

**What would not reopen it: anything on the ordinary list.** Growth would not. A partner's withdrawal would not. The observation that a charter would be cheaper, easier, or more competitive would not — that observation is precisely what moved Revolut and Monzo, and pre-committing against it is the entire function of writing this down.

**What would reopen it is evidence that the refusal fails at what it is meant to do.** This paper claims that staying off the charter buys three things: the gratitude record, not the money, stays at the center of the institution (§4); its economics keep circulating rather than accumulating (§3); and its executive seat stays free to pass to Miss Aquarius, with no human executive answerable to a regulator for money the institution holds (§2). Evidence that HeartBank, while holding no one's money, has drifted onto the money anyway — that its attention, its products and its income have moved from the record to the funds, so that the refusal is protecting nothing — would reopen the question. So would evidence that the refusal does not, in fact, keep the executive seat free: if the places HeartBank serves came to demand the same human executive succession of any institution that merely records payments, the refusal would no longer buy what it was adopted to buy. Either finding would oblige the institution to re-examine the commitment honestly rather than defend it at greater length.

**The asymmetry is deliberate.** What reopens this commitment is evidence about whether the refusal works; evidence about what it costs never does. The costs are set out in §5.2 and were accepted when the commitment was made, and a commitment that reopens on its cost was never binding. The form follows *HeartBank's Position: The Moat Is What We Refuse* §7.5, which states it for the institution's refusals as a class.

**What the institution will concede is a question of fact rather than of pressure.** If a jurisdiction concluded that HeartBank's activity constitutes deposit-taking as a matter of law, the institution's obligation would be to change *the activity* so that it does not — narrowing the product, restructuring the flow, or withdrawing from that jurisdiction — rather than to accept the charter. **The commitment is to not being that kind of institution, not to a particular reading of a statute.** If the only lawful way to keep operating in a place were to become a chartered bank, HeartBank would leave that place. That is a real and costly consequence and the institution accepts it here, in advance, in writing.

## 5.5 · What makes a self-binding worth anything

The no-charter commitment is one of several permanent refusals the institution has published, each in its own position paper — never a banking charter, never a patent, never advertising revenue, and never the sale, licensing, rental or disclosure of any person's records — and one member of a wider set of refusals listed together in *HeartBank's Position: The Moat Is What We Refuse*. That paper argues the class: why a costly, public, permanent refusal is worth having at all (its §2), and what would and would not reopen one (its §7.5). This section does not repeat that argument. It adds what is specific to binding oneself: the properties that make a published refusal more than a sentence, and how far this institution's refusals have them today.

**These refusals share a shape: each closes a door that would be profitable to open.** That is not incidental; it is the only kind of door worth closing in advance. Nobody needs to pre-commit against doing something unattractive. A commitment is worth exactly what it would cost to keep at the moment it is tested, and each of these is designed to be tested by success — the charter becomes attractive at scale, the patent becomes attractive once a mechanism has value, the advertising becomes attractive once there is attention to sell, and the sale of records becomes attractive once there is a record worth buying. **An institution that binds itself only against things it does not want to do has bound itself against nothing.**

The lineage is the pre-commitment literature — Ulysses ordering himself tied to the mast, and its formal treatment in Schelling's work on strategic commitment and Elster's on binding oneself. The insight those share is that **a constraint is credible in proportion to how hard it is to undo**, and that removing one's own future options can be a source of strength rather than a loss of freedom, because it changes what others can expect and what one's own successors can be pressured into.

Four properties make a self-binding more than a sentence, and the institution states them so it can be held to them: the commitment must be **public**, so reversal is visible; **dated**, so drift is measurable; it must **name what would, and would not, reopen it**, so that the ordinary pressures are pre-answered rather than freshly argued each time, and the one kind of reason that should count is stated in advance; and it must eventually be **structurally enforced** rather than merely stated, so that it does not depend on the character of whoever holds the institution next.

⚠️ **On the fourth property the institution is not yet where it claims to be, and says so.** These commitments are today published intentions backed by the founder's word and by the timestamped public record. The instruments that would make them structurally binding — the governing purpose of the trust that will own the institution, and the entity structure beneath it — are drafted or pending, not executed. **Until they are, a determined successor could reverse any of them, and the only real cost would be the embarrassment of a public reversal against a dated record.** That is a meaningful cost and it is not the same as impossibility. The institution regards closing that gap as the most important unfinished governance work it has, and would rather a reader hold it to that than believe the bindings are already load-bearing.

## 6 · Why this is bound now, and offered as a pattern

HeartBank records the non-bank position as a permanent constraint for the same reason it binds its other permanent refusals: an option left open is an option a future steward can be pressured to exercise. An institution that holds the *possibility* of a banking charter will, under growth pressure, eventually hear the argument that chartering would be simpler, or more legitimate, or more lucrative.

By binding the position now, HeartBank ensures that its intended autonomous successor inherits a settled architecture, and is never placed in the position of being the regulated custodian of other people's deposits — a category of legal and existential exposure the institution has chosen, deliberately, never to enter. The pattern is offered under CC0 — a public-domain dedication: anyone may use it, for anything, without asking — to any institution, particularly any built for autonomous-AI succession, that needs value to move between people at large scale without becoming the value's custodian and being regulated as one.

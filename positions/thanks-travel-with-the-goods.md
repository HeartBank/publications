# HeartBank's Position: Thanks Travel With the Goods

**A Thank-You Sticker Inside the Package, for Orders That Never Pass Through an App: What HeartBank Believes It Can Do, What It Cannot Yet Show, and How One Village Will Test It**

---

## Executive Summary

Many small sellers already put a thank-you inside the order: a note, a label with their photo and phone number, sometimes a short video. Nobody asks them to. It is a thank-you that travels with the goods.

HeartBank's position is narrow. **A small sticker in the package, which opens the seller's own thank-you and which the seller can add as a gift for the customer's own gift, gives two people a quiet way to thank the shop after the goods arrive: the person who bought them, and the person they were given to.** That is the whole claim. This paper explains why it is smaller than "thank-after commerce", which is where the idea began, and what it would take to show more.

What the paper does **not** claim: that paying or thanking after the goods already works (no deployment has shown it); that a sticker fixes the reason many people in Cambodia pay on delivery; that thanks will spread from home to home; or that money will move between neighbors because of it. Those are bets. HeartBank wrote them down as seven predictions, publicly and before the first sticker ships, to be read once after the season that ends on 7 January 2028, in one village.

The commitments in §11 hold whatever the result.

---

## 1 · A bag on a doorstep

A seller in a Cambodian village packs an order: two shirts, a bag of dried mango, a bottle of fish sauce. On the outside she sticks a big handmade label. It has her photo, her phone number, the code people scan to pay her, and a line in her own hand. Sometimes she films a short thank-you and sends it to the buyer.

None of this is a feature anyone sold her. It is how she says thank you.

Now follow the bag. The buyer opens it and is pleased. There is no easy way to say so back, except a message she may or may not send. And suppose the shirts were a birthday present. The person who ends up wearing them never knew the shop at all. The seller's thanks reached one person; nothing came back, and nothing went further.

That small gap is what this paper is about.

---

## 2 · What "after" means here, said carefully

The idea began as a bigger one: *thank-after commerce*, where the goods come first and the thank, or even the payment, comes after. HeartBank holds to part of that, and has to be precise about which part.

**The price still binds before.** On an ordinary priced item, the buyer pays the price. A thank that comes afterwards is gratitude, in words, and money only if the buyer wishes to add it. On a priced item, money after the goods is a tip. Tipping is not a common habit in Cambodia, and this paper does not pretend otherwise.

**Paying after is real only on a "thank-after" item.** HeartBank's shop software lets a seller mark an item to be thanked for afterwards: the buyer receives it first and is asked only then. Such items are still rare, and whether they work is, as our own earlier research put it, *an empirical question that no deployment has yet answered.*

**Delivery stays paid in advance.** In HeartBank® Shops, a delivered order is paid before it leaves: the rider carries goods and never money, so nobody riding alone holds cash and nobody stands at a gate waiting to be paid. A gift sent to someone else is meant to be paid first too, so the person receiving it is not asked to pay. A sticker in the package changes none of this. **It does not move a payment to after; it brings back the thank.**

**Inside the app, the buyer already has a door.** When an order goes through HeartBank Shops, the buyer can be offered a chance to thank the shop after the hand-over. The sticker adds something only where that screen never reaches:

```
                         who can thank the shop after the goods arrive?
                         ──────────────────────────────────────────────
                         the buyer                 the gift's recipient
   order in the app      yes (the thank screen)    no
   order by phone/chat   only by sending a message no
   + a sticker inside    yes                       yes
```

So the claim is about two people the app cannot reach: **a buyer whose order was taken by phone, chat or social media, and anyone who receives the goods as a gift.**

## 3 · The claim

A B-Stamp™ is a small purple sticker with a code a phone can scan. Scanning it opens a page with a giver's words, sometimes their voice, and a promise of time together. A family has used it on presents for more than a year. This paper is about a seller using it.

In the plan, the seller puts **two** stamps in an order:

```
   the shop ──[stamp 1: the shop's own thank-you]──────────▶ the buyer
       │
       └──[stamp 2: a free stamp, the shop's gift]──▶ the buyer's own gift
                                                        │
                                                        ▼
                                         the person who receives it opens it:
                                           1. the giver's message, first
                                           2. a promise of time together
                                           3. "from <the shop>", once, in small print
                                           4. a way to thank the shop, if they wish
```

The first stamp carries the seller's thanks to the buyer, and lets the buyer thank back in their own time. The second is the seller's gift to the buyer, to use on a present of their own. Whoever receives that present sees the giver's words first; the shop appears once, below, as the place the stamp came from; and the recipient may thank the shop, in words, and with money only if they choose.

The seller may link the two stamps, so the shop can see the small loop it started: its own thank-you, the gift that went on, and any thanks that came back. It shows only the thanks their authors chose to make public, and it shows them as the most recent, never as a count.

In a village where many homes also sell something, the loop can continue. The person who received the gift may run a shop too, and put stamps in their own orders:

```
   supplier ──▶ #shop ──▶ @customer ──▶ @loved one ──▶ back to #shop
                                            │
                                            └─▶ their own shop ──▶ the next circle
```

That circle is the hope. It is not a finding. §9 says how it will be tested.

## 4 · Why the shop's name on someone else's gift is not an advertisement

HeartBank has said publicly that it *will not turn a gift into an advertising surface*, and that it takes no money for *sponsored placements* or for *directing a person toward a seller*. A reader is entitled to ask whether a shop's name on someone else's present breaks that.

HeartBank's answer is that the second stamp **is the shop's gift**. The shop gave it to the buyer to give on. Naming the shop names a giver, the way a present can say who made it. What would make it an advertisement, and is ruled out:

- an offer, a discount, a coupon, or *visit us*, anywhere on someone else's gift;
- the shop's name anywhere but once, in small print, below the giver's own words;
- a shop paying to have its name shown to more people. A seller may print stamps at no cost or order them for the cost of shipping, and a business that makes them in bulk through HeartBank's software pays only what making them costs, never more for wider reach;
- any count of how many people saw a given shop's name. HeartBank does not compute one.

The giver can also hide the shop's name before giving. It is their gift.

---

## 5 · The treat for two

A shop may add one more thing to the free stamp: its own small treat, for when the giver and the person who received the gift come in together. It is the shop's offer, from its own menu, at its own cost. Both people check in at the shop, and the treat is theirs.

What it never does: it never says *free*, it never asks anyone to bring a friend, it never counts down, and it never reminds anyone. It is stated once.

**A concession:** a treat that needs two people does invite two people, whatever the words say. HeartBank accepts that, because time together, in person, is the point. It also means the treat cannot double as proof of time together; §9 says so where it is measured.

## 6 · The strongest objection

**People in Cambodia often pay on delivery to protect themselves, not out of a custom of thanking after.** A U.S. government guide to e-commerce in Cambodia, published in 2025, says that *most transactions are settled through cash-on-delivery, raising concerns about security and costs* ([trade.gov](https://trade.gov/country-commercial-guides/cambodia-ecommerce)). There are many reports of buyers losing money they paid in advance to online sellers who then vanished ([Open Development Cambodia](https://opendevelopmentcambodia.net/news/cambodians-report-scams-in-online-shopping-experiences/)). Paying on delivery answers one question: *will the goods come, and will they be what I ordered?*

A thank-you sticker does nothing for that question. HeartBank carries this objection rather than answering it:

- The sticker is not a trust mechanism and is never presented as one.
- HeartBank's own shop software asks for payment before delivery, for the rider's safety. A buyer who wants the protection of paying on delivery is, today, not served by it. That is a real cost of the rider's safety, and it is stated here.
- "Goods first, payment after" is how much counter trade already works in Cambodia, between people who know each other. That is a fact about the market. It is not evidence that thanking after works with strangers.

A second objection is narrower but serious. **A card with a QR code inside a parcel is a known scam.** U.S. postal inspectors warn of unexpected packages holding a small gift and a QR card that leads to a fake website ([USPIS](https://www.uspis.gov/news/scam-article/quishing)). A person who never bought from the shop is being asked to scan a code from it. Every stamp will open a page at a HeartBank address that a careful person can check first, but a careful person may still decline to scan it. HeartBank considers that caution right, and asks nothing that would talk anyone out of it.

## 7 · What others have tried, including what did not last

- **Gift messages by QR code are already common.** Online-store apps such as VideoGreet put a code on the gift or its packing slip so the person receiving it can watch the sender's video message. The first half of this paper's sticker, a code in the package that opens a message, is ordinary.
- **A receiver thanking by scan already exists.** Amazon's gift receipt has carried a code that lets the person who received a gift send a thank-you note to the person who sent it ([International Business Times, 2015](https://www.ibtimes.com/amazon-beefs-gift-receipt-service-automated-thank-you-notes-2228033)). There, the thanks go to the giver. Here, they may also go to the shop.
- **Refer-a-friend cards** tucked into boxes pass a discount to a friend. They carry a price, not a thank-you, and many are never used.
- **Paying after has failed before.** Panera Cares, a chain of pay-what-you-can cafés in the United States where people decided what to pay, closed its last café in February 2019, calling it no longer viable ([Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2019-02-04/panera-will-end-its-pay-what-you-want-test-closing-boston-store)).

HeartBank ran a quick search of its own before writing this, and found that every piece of the sticker already exists somewhere. Whether the whole loop has been tried, it could not say: the check it relied on did not pass its own test. So this paper claims no novelty.

## 8 · What has been done, and what has not

**Done:**
- One family has used B-Stamps on presents for more than a year, by choice.
- One home seller has been seen, unprompted, putting a thank-you in every order.
- Seven predictions about the village test were written down publicly before the first stamp (§9).

**Not done:**
- No seller has put a B-Stamp in an order. Ordering and printing stamps at heartbank.com are not built yet.
- Nobody outside that family has used the thank-back.
- Whether Cambodian families put notes or labels on gifts at all is unknown. Money in an envelope is a common gift at many occasions; that is a different question, and this paper is not about it.

---

## 9 · What would prove this wrong

The test is one village, for one season, from the first stamp to **7 January 2028**, read **once** after it closes. Every prediction is scored across the village, never for one shop. None is scored in money from thanks: HeartBank stores no amount for a thank, so giving and declining look the same, on purpose. The stamp's page may offer the chance to thank or to give the free stamp on; nothing may prompt either, and a prompt would spoil the test.

| ID | What we predict | What would prove it wrong |
|---|---|---|
| **P-VL1** | At least 10% of the free stamps shops include are used by the customer on a gift of their own | fewer: the customer step does not ride a gift habit here |
| **P-VL2** | At least 5% of those gifts' recipients thank the shop within 30 days | fewer: thanking the shop is not something a recipient does here |
| **P-VL3** | At least 10% of stamps carrying a shop's treat end with the two people checking in together | fewer: the sticker carries thanks, not people together. *This counts the treat being used by two people, the closest instrument we have; it is not time together itself.* |
| **P-VL4** | At least one supplier puts stamps in its deliveries to a village shop | none: the circle has no upstream |
| **P-VL5** | At least one person who received a gift starts putting stamps in orders from a shop of their own | none: the circle does not reproduce |
| **P-VL6** | Shops start in an order set by a draw no one controls; a shop's monthly orders from other village shops, recorded through HeartBank Shops, rise after it starts by at least 20% more than in shops that have not started | a smaller gap, or none: the stamp does not move money between shops |
| **P-VL7** | At least 10% of orders that stamp-issuing shops receive come from buyers who first reached the shop through a stamp's link | fewer: the stamp rarely brings a shop a new buyer |

**P-VL6 needs a warning of its own.** It counts money recorded through HeartBank's software, never all money in the village. A shop that starts recording purchases it used to settle in cash would raise the number without any new money moving. The read will say so.

**What a pass would and would not show.** One village, one season, with the founder living there and the first stamps coming from a shop he knows well: a pass would earn a second village without him. It would not be a claim about Cambodia. If P-VL2 and P-VL3 both fail, HeartBank will say that the circle described in §3 did not happen, and will publish that as plainly as a pass.

## 10 · Our stake

- **HeartBank makes the stamps and gives them away.** A seller may print them at no cost, or order them for the cost of shipping.
- **The founder will be living in the village during the test,** and the first stamps will come from a shop he knows well. That is a confound, stated rather than hidden: his presence alone could make people more willing to try.
- **HeartBank earns from people holding a name in its registry,** paid yearly; a shop's own tag stays free, and HeartBank's other charges follow what things cost. People who meet a stamp may later choose to hold a name. So HeartBank has a commercial interest in stamps traveling widely, and this paper should be read with that in mind.

---

## 11 · What HeartBank commits to, whatever the result

- **The sticker never replaces thanking someone in person.** It is there for when the person is not.
- **The giver's words come first.** The shop is named once, in small print, and never with an offer.
- **No ratings, no stars, no ranking of shops, and no count of anyone's thanks.** Public thanks, where their authors choose to share them, appear as the most recent, never as a number.
- **Nobody is prompted** to thank, to pass a stamp on, or to bring a friend.
- **Delivery stays paid in advance;** the rider never carries money.
- **No one needs an account to open a stamp.**
- **Success is never measured in money given as thanks.** HeartBank does not store it.

**What would reopen this position:**
- If the season's read shows that recipients do not thank shops (P-VL2) and pairs do not come in (P-VL3), the circle in §3 is withdrawn as a claim, and this paper says so in a new version.
- If any way appears for a business to pay for its name to reach more people, §4 is reopened before that way ships.
- If the treat for two becomes the reason people come, rather than the person they came with, §5 is reopened.

## 12 · Relation to the mechanism, and what comes next

The sticker itself, a gift tag whose parts can stay sealed until a chosen moment and which carries a promise of time together, is specified in *The Time-Locked Gift Tag* ([`gift-tag-time-reveal`](https://thonly.org/research/mechanism/gift-tag-time-reveal)). A section on the seller-issued form will be added there. This paper does not re-argue the mechanism; it states what HeartBank believes about one use of it, and how that belief will be checked.

After the village season, HeartBank will publish a white paper reporting what happened, including every prediction that failed.

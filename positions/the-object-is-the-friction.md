# HeartBank's Position: The Object Is the Friction

**Why Gift-Giving Is the Last Domain Where a Physical Object Remains Culturally Compulsory — and Why the Compulsion Is Friction Rather Than Preference**

**Keywords:** gift economy, gift-giving, experiential purchases, material purchases, time as a gift, non-fungible time, costly signalling, gift friction, consumer culture, gratitude infrastructure, position paper.

> **Status: candidate position under validation.** See §7. Published CC0; the argument rests on Van Boven & Gilovich (2003) and the Harvard Study of Adult Development, and the gift-domain extension is the untested part.

---

## Executive Summary

Across most of modern life, people have been free to choose between giving a thing and giving an experience, and the evidence on which choice produces more lasting satisfaction has been consistent for two decades. **Gift-giving is the exception.** At a birthday, at a wedding, at a holiday table, arriving without an object is still read as arriving without a gift.

This paper argues that the persistence of the object at those moments is **not a preference people hold but a friction they are subject to** — and that the friction is specific, nameable, and removable. What has never been practically giftable is **the giver's own time**: there has been no way to hand it over, no artifact to place in someone's hands, no moment at which it becomes real to both parties. HeartBank's position is that supplying that artifact does not add a new kind of gift to the existing set. It removes an obstruction, and what is behind the obstruction is what the evidence says people wanted.

The paper also states what the institution will *not* do with this argument, because the most obvious commercial use of it is one we refuse.

## 1 · The observation this position began from

The institution's attention was drawn to this by an ordinary scene: a family birthday, a table of wrapped objects, and the recognition that several people present had arrived with something purchased not because a purchased thing was what they most wanted to give, but because it was the only form in which a gift could be handed over.

That is a small observation and it does not prove anything. It is recorded here because it is the origin of the position and because the position should be readable as what it is — a hypothesis about a cultural mechanism, arrived at by noticing, and argued below against the evidence that exists.

## 2 · The claim

**In gift-giving, the object is friction, not preference.**

Three parts, in order of how contestable each is:

1. **The object is compulsory at gift occasions in a way it is not elsewhere.** This is descriptive and we take it as uncontroversial: a guest at a birthday who says *"I didn't bring anything, but I'd like to spend a day with you"* is doing something socially legible but unusual, and is likely to feel the need to explain.
2. **The compulsion is not tracking what people most want to give or receive.** This is where the evidence enters (§4).
3. **The compulsion persists because the alternative has not been handed-over-able.** This is the claim that is ours, and the one this position rests on.

## 3 · The adversary: experience gifts exist, and they did not displace the object

The obvious objection is that the alternative *has* existed for decades. Gift certificates, restaurant vouchers, concert tickets, spa days, cooking classes, and an entire platform category of bookable experiences are all purchasable, giftable, and widely marketed. If the object were merely friction, this industry should have dissolved it.

It did not. **The wrapped object survived the experience-gift industry essentially untouched**, and we take that failure seriously rather than treating it as a marketing problem.

Our reading of why is the centre of this paper. **An experience gift is still a purchase — it buys a third party's time.** The recipient of a restaurant voucher receives an evening staffed by strangers; the recipient of a class receives an instructor's afternoon. Something real is given, and the giver's own hours are not in it. The experience-gift industry did not test the proposition that people would rather receive time than objects. It tested whether people would rather receive *purchased services* than objects, which is a different proposition and one that objects can win.

**What has never been giftable is the giver's own time**, and the reason is structural rather than commercial: a person's hours are non-fungible and cannot be transferred. Only that person can spend them, which means a gift of one's time is not a transfer at all but a **commitment**, and commitments have historically had no artifact. There has been nothing to wrap, nothing to hand across a table, and nothing that survives the evening as evidence that the commitment was made.

## 4 · What the evidence supports, and what it does not

Two findings bear on this position, and neither was produced to test it.

**Experiential purchases produce more enduring satisfaction than material ones.** The finding originates with Van Boven and Gilovich (2003) and has been extended and qualified repeatedly since; the durable core is that experiences resist adaptation and comparison better than possessions do, and are more readily integrated into a person's sense of who they are.

**Relationship quality is among the strongest available predictors of late-life health and wellbeing.** The Harvard Study of Adult Development, following its cohorts across more than eight decades, has reported this consistently.

⚠️ **What these findings do not establish is the gift case, and we state that plainly.** Both study what people acquire *for themselves*. The gift is a different structure: a giver spends money on an object for someone else, under social observation, at a dated occasion, with the recipient's reaction as the immediate feedback. **The gift is the under-studied corner**, and it is precisely the corner where the object has remained compulsory. Our position extends the established finding into that corner; the extension is the untested part and is treated as such in §7.

We also note what this position does not rest on. It does not rest on any of the widely circulated lists of the regrets of the dying, which are palliative-care memoir rather than research and are frequently cited as though they were data. The argument stands on the two findings above or it does not stand.

## 5 · The strongest objection, and what we concede

The best argument for the object is not sentimental. It is this:

> **An object is a costly signal that has already been paid. A pledge of time is a promise that has not.**

The purchased gift arrives with its sacrifice complete — money spent, time taken to choose it, the thing itself sitting there as proof. A commitment of future hours arrives with its sacrifice entirely ahead of it, and the recipient is asked to accept an intention. Between a completed sacrifice and an intended one, a rational recipient may reasonably prefer the former, and a giver who wishes to be believed may reasonably prefer to be seen paying.

**We concede that this is true, and that it is not fully answerable.** Three features of the design narrow it rather than closing it: the pledged quantity is **set by the institution's AI rather than by the giver**, so it cannot be inflated to impress; honouring it requires **both parties to be present at the same time**, so it cannot be discharged cheaply or at a distance; and the pledge is **announced aloud, in the room, at the moment of giving**, which converts it into a public commitment witnessed by everyone there.

That last feature is doing the most work, and it is worth saying so directly: **the ceremony is not decoration. For a gift with no object, the ceremony is the costly signal.** A pledge read out among family is harder to abandon than one made privately, and its cost is reputational rather than monetary. This is weaker than a purchased object's proof, and we do not claim otherwise. It is the residue of the objection that survives our answer.

## 6 · What the institution refuses

The most obvious commercial application of this argument is one we will not make.

A gift that requires no purchase is available to people who cannot purchase — which includes the poor, and includes **every child**, who has no money by life stage rather than by circumstance. That reach is real and it is one of the reasons this work matters.

⛔ **HeartBank will not market a time-gift as the option for people who cannot afford presents.** Not in advertising, not in onboarding copy, not as a tier label, not as a segment in a deck. A product that introduces itself by naming what its user lacks has made the user's poverty the first thing the product says about them, and no amount of downstream warmth repairs that.

**The reach is a consequence of the design and never its framing.** Where the institution leads instead: with the child giving an afternoon to a grandparent, and with the recipients for whom a gift of time together is not a substitute for a better gift but plainly the better gift — those who are lonely, those at a distance, those whose relationships have thinned. **This is a standing commitment and it is published here so that it can be held against us.**

## 7 · What would prove this wrong

This position is stated as a **candidate under validation**, in the same posture as the institution's position on future kindness as an operating noun, and it is published before the evidence rather than after because a position taken only once it is safe is not a position.

**The pre-registered prediction.** At a gift occasion where both forms are present, a bare time-gift will be **recalled more often, and rated as more meaningful, at three and twelve months** than an object gift of comparable ceremonial weight given at the same occasion to the same recipient.

**The instrument** is the December 2026 holiday season — the first occasion at which the artifact and the practice both exist. The sample will be small, familial, and disclosed as such.

**What would falsify the position:** if recipients recall and value object gifts equally or more; if givers who used the bare form report having felt they gave *less*; or if the form is taken up only by people who could not have afforded an object, which would indicate that we have built a budget substitute and described it as a preference.

**We will publish the result either way**, including if it contradicts this paper.

## 8 · Relation to the mechanism

The artifact this position depends on is specified elsewhere and is not re-argued here: a gift tag whose fields reveal on the occasion, carrying a pledge of the giver's time, given with or without anything attached to it. The unattached configuration — the tag given alone, the pledge as the gift itself — is specified in *The Time-Locked Gift Tag*, §4.10. **This paper argues why that configuration matters; that paper specifies how it works.**

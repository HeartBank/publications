## Executive Summary

The political economy of giving has long been argued between two poles. On one, **coerced redistribution** corrects the distribution from above, by compulsory transfer. On the other, **pure accumulation** preserves liberty over holdings and never asks for a transfer at all. HeartBank's institutional position is that there is a third position, orthogonal to both, that neither pole can occupy: **voluntary circulation** — relinquishment freely chosen, measured, and mirrored back to the giver.

The position rests on one load-bearing claim: *the relinquishment HeartBank cultivates cannot be coerced.* A gift compelled from outside produces the transfer without the free volition that makes a gift a gift — and, by a well-established body of evidence on motivation, compulsion and payment tend to *crowd out* the very disposition they are meant to support. It follows that the institution can only ever provide the **arena** in which the free choice is available and the **mirror** by which a participant reads their own progress — never the lever. And it follows that HeartBank requires a host society that leaves the disposal of one's own holdings free, because the free choice not to accumulate is virtuous, and measurable, only where accumulation was genuinely available. The institution is therefore the voluntary-circulation layer a free order structurally lacks: not a redistributive mechanism, not a neutral marketplace, but the valve that completes a free order from within.

## 1. The two poles

Coerced redistribution achieves a distribution but cannot grow a disposition: a transfer extracted by compulsion is not generosity, however just the resulting pattern. Pure accumulation preserves liberty but offers no valve: care remains a private surplus, expressed if at all by individual whim, with no infrastructure to make the giving easy, dignified, or visible-to-oneself. Each pole is answering a real concern of the other — distribution, or liberty — and neither addresses the thing HeartBank exists to grow: the freely-chosen, cultivated practice of circulation.

## 2. The third position

HeartBank is the voluntary-circulation layer. It does not compel a transfer (the redistributive move) and it does not merely host a market (the accumulation move). It provides an arena in which giving is structurally easy and a mirror in which a participant can see their own movement from holding toward circulating — and then it leaves the choice entirely free. It completes a free order rather than replacing it, by adding from within, voluntarily, the circulation that order does not supply on its own.

## 3. Why it cannot be coerced

The claim that the relinquishment cannot be coerced is not sentiment; it is structural. What the institution values is a cultivated interior disposition — generosity freely chosen — and a coerced relinquishment yields the outward fact (the transfer) while voiding the interior one (the free volition). The contemplative analysis the institution's substrate carries is explicit on this: it is the volition that makes an act meritorious, not the transfer alone. And the empirical literature on motivation converges: paying for or compelling prosocial behavior frequently *crowds out* the intrinsic disposition, converting a relation of care into a priced or policed transaction (the classic findings on paid versus voluntary blood donation, and on fines that became prices, are the canonical cases). Coercion does not approximate the target imperfectly; it destroys the thing while producing its shadow.

## 4. What this means for the host society

If the relinquishment must be free, the institution requires a host in which *not* relinquishing is a live, real option — because the virtue and the measurement both depend on the choice being free. This is a narrow claim and the institution states it narrowly, against the loose version that would misread it. HeartBank does **not** hold that any particular accumulation order is good, that inequality should be endorsed, or that redistribution is wrong. It holds only that a society leaving the disposal of one's own holdings free is the necessary *arena* for the cultivated, measurable relinquishment the institution grows. Virtue measured is virtue uncompelled.

## 5. The architecture already encodes it

The position is not aspirational; it is built. The institution rule-empties its **transient communal vessels** — the earmarked re-tip pools that exist to be passed onward — on a fixed annual cadence; but this is not coercion, because depositing into such a vessel *is itself the free act*, and the emptying only enforces a commitment the participant already made. The institution **never** rule-empties a participant's **personal account**: its return toward zero is left entirely to the participant's free circulation. Freedom applies to what is yours; the institution may hold a freely-made commitment to its purpose, but it may never seize a holding. The line between the two vessels is the third path made structural.

## 6. What this position is not, and the misreading it invites

This paper argues in a vocabulary that is politically loaded, and it will be read by people who have a side. The institution states plainly what it is not saying.

**It takes no position on tax policy, welfare provision, or the proper size of the state.** §3's claim is narrow and specific: *the disposition HeartBank cultivates cannot be produced by compulsion*, because a transfer extracted under compulsion is not the thing being grown. **That is a claim about what this institution can do, not a claim about what a government should do.** A society may tax, redistribute and provide at any level it chooses, and every word of this position remains true inside it — the free choice not to accumulate remains available to anyone with anything left to choose about.

**"HeartBank requires a host society that leaves the disposal of one's own holdings free" is a statement of operating condition, not of political preference.** Read as advocacy it becomes an argument against redistribution, and that reading is wrong: the institution does not compete with public provision and does not claim to substitute for it. **It occupies a seat the state cannot occupy, for the same reason it does not occupy the state's.**

**And it is not a claim that voluntary circulation is sufficient.** §7 says why it is not.

## 7. The honest limits, and the objection that has the better of us

**Voluntary circulation cannot guarantee a floor. That is the whole reason coerced redistribution exists**, and this position does not answer it. A system that depends on freely-chosen giving reaches people in proportion to the disposition and surplus of others, which is exactly the property that makes the giving meaningful and exactly the property that makes it unreliable for anyone whose survival is at stake. **An institution built on voluntary circulation cannot promise anybody anything**, and should never be offered to a policymaker as an alternative to provision that can.

**The strongest published objection is Rob Reich's**, and it lands. *Just Giving* argues that private philanthropy is structurally undemocratic — that it lets the preferences of those with surplus determine which needs are met, without accountability to those whose needs they are, and that a liberal society should be suspicious of it rather than grateful for it. **Nothing in HeartBank's design escapes that critique.** A gratitude economy still routes resources according to who was noticed, and being noticed is not distributed fairly. The institution's mitigations — the funded floor that does not depend on being seen, the refusal to rank recipients, the anonymity options — reduce the problem and do not dissolve it. *We think Reich is describing a real defect in the thing we are building, and we would rather say so than pretend the gift frame answers him.*

**Titmuss cuts both ways and is usually cited only on the half that flatters us.** *The Gift Relationship* is the canonical demonstration that voluntary donation can outperform a market — and its author's conclusion was an argument for *public provision*, not for a third sector. An institution citing Titmuss for the crowding-out mechanism should also report that he did not draw this paper's conclusion from it.

## 8. Prior art — and the thinker whose thesis this most resembles

**Elinor Ostrom is the unavoidable precedent, and this paper should have led with her.** *Governing the Commons* and the work that earned the 2009 Nobel established, empirically and against the prevailing consensus, that there is a durable third way between market and state: communities governing common-pool resources through self-organised rules, monitoring and graduated sanctions, sustained over generations. **"There is a third position orthogonal to both poles" is Ostrom's finding before it is ours**, and any claim to originality here would be false.

⭐ **The difference worth stating is what is being governed.** Ostrom's commons are *rivalrous* resources — fisheries, forests, irrigation systems — where the problem is depletion and the solution is a rule structure preventing overuse. HeartBank's subject is a *disposition*, which is not depleted by use and in fact grows with it, so the governance problem inverts: the institution is not preventing overuse of a scarce thing but attempting to occasion more use of an inexhaustible one. **Ostrom's design principles were written for a problem of restraint; this is a problem of invitation.** Whether her principles transfer to that inversion is an open question this institution has not answered, and we flag it as the most promising unexplored connection in the corpus.

**The other members of the lineage** — Mauss on gift circulation, Polanyi on the disembedding of economy from society, and the mutual-aid and cooperative traditions — are already carried elsewhere in the research corpus and are not restated here.

## 9. What would, and would not, change this

**Nothing on the ordinary list.** Political criticism from either pole would not. An argument that compulsion would be faster would not — speed was never the claim.

**What would change it is evidence about crowding-out running the other way.** The position rests on the claim that a cultivated disposition is displaced by compulsion and payment. If it turned out that measured, mirrored, voluntary circulation *also* crowds out the disposition it means to grow — that making giving visible to oneself corrupts it in the way an external reward does — then the arena-and-mirror design is not a third path but a subtler version of the second pole, and the institution would need to say so and rebuild. **That is a testable proposition and the institution has pre-registered its own version of it.**

## Closing

HeartBank's position is that the most important transfers cannot be commanded into existence and should not be left to chance — they must be *invited*, made easy, and reflected back to the giver, in a society that leaves the giver free to refuse. That is the third path: not the state's compulsion and not the market's silence, but the freely-chosen circulation that completes a free order from within. The institution builds the arena and holds up the mirror, and then — even at the cost of a lower number — it leaves the choice free.
